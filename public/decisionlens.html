DecisionLens

When a model says no,
what can an explanation tell us?

DomainGraduate admissionsMethodsRandom forests + DiCEStatusResearch prototype

The problem

A prediction alone gives an applicant little insight into a decision. Counterfactual explanations suggest changes that could alter that prediction. But whether those suggestions are useful depends on what the applicant can change, the costs involved, and the assumptions made about how features relate.

DecisionLens explores these questions through a graduate-admissions prototype, motivated by my experience of applying to graduate programmes.

What I built

The implementation combines a random forest classifier with DiCE counterfactual generation. It compares baseline suggestions with suggestions weighted by assumed feature costs, then examines downstream adjustments under two manually specified causal scenarios.

An applicant interface presents suggested changes and model outcomes. The project also explores how recourse validity varies across groups defined by academic background and research experience.

How the prototype works

  1. Derive a binary target by applying a 0.75 threshold to the dataset’s “Chance of Admit” field.
  2. Train a random forest with an 80/20 stratified split and a five-fold cross-validation procedure.
  3. Generate baseline and feature-cost-weighted counterfactuals with DiCE’s genetic method.
  4. Apply scenario-based downstream adjustments and check the resulting model prediction.
The target is derived from a dataset field. It does not represent observed decisions from an admissions committee.

Assumptions and limitations

The causal scenarios and action costs are manually specified. They are hypotheses for exploration, not empirically established causal relationships or measured applicant costs.

The interface contains both connected prototype components and demonstration data. The project does not establish guaranteed admission, optimal recourse, demographic fairness, or real-world deployment readiness.

My access to institutional data and admissions decision processes was limited. That limitation is central to the research question: how should we communicate advice when the model and its assumptions are uncertain?

Research direction

I want to evaluate how explanation and recourse methods behave under different feasibility constraints, and how people interpret the limits of their recommendations. A reproducible evaluation with documented baselines and failure cases is the next step.
