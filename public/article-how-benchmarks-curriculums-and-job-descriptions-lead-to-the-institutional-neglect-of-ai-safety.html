How Benchmarks, Curriculums and Job Descriptions Lead to the Institutional Neglect of AI Safety

AI safety is not trailing because we forgot it; it is trailing because we structurally deprioritized it. What we measure in benchmarks, teach in classrooms, and reward in hiring quietly determines what gets built.

Jabess Omane 2026-06-02 · 3 min read

These three words rarely appear in the same sentence: benchmarks, curriculums, and job descriptions. Only after connecting the dots did I come to the realisation of how interconnected they are and their influence on AI Safety. That discovery changed how I think about the AI safety debate. 

Everyone keeps asking why AI safety is lagging behind AI capability. Wrong question. A better one is this: What exactly did we optimize the ecosystem to produce?

Let me use one simple analogy.

Think about a football (what Messi and Ronaldo play) academy. If the academy trains players only to score goals, scouts for the best attackers and awards player of the match awards to top goal scorers per match or training session you should not be shocked when the team concedes too many goals.

You cannot build a system that rewards attack only, then cry later that defense is weak.

That is exactly what we are doing in AI. 

In AI benchmarks(LLM's specifically), we what we evaluate them by is like the case of football it's attacking prowess. In other words; toekns, coding perfomance etc. We seek to reward technical capability. Higher accuracy. Better output. Faster systems. Safety is there, but often in small print. So teams optimize for what is visible: performance. That is normal human behavior. If safety is not on the main scoreboard, safety will not be the main work.

Then  you take a look our our machine learning curriculumns. Many AI courses still treat safety and ethics like side dishes. Important, yes. But not core. So people can finish strong technical training without deep practice in recourse, harm review, and accountability in real institutions. They graduate with execellent coding skills and all that but very little intentionality in ethics and safety.

Then hiring finishes the cycle. Open most AI/ML job posts and you will see strong focus on tools, speed, and shipping. Very little clear focus on safety thinking, contestability, and incident responsibility. Job descriptions are not neutral. They tell people what matters. If companies do not ask for safety skills, courses treat them as 'add-ons' and teams will not prioritize safety work.


It seems we've created a pattern where;


Benchmarks shape what researchers chase.
Curriculums define what engineers learn.
Job descriptions shape what  field treats as competence.

That combination shapes what gets deployed to society.

This is why I say the AI safety gap is not just a technical problem. It is a design problem in our institutions. We built a pipeline that rewards capability first and safety later. Then we act surprised when safety comes later. 

My point is simple: safety should not be a patch. It should be a design choice from day one.

If we can agree that AI systems affect real lives, then safety cannot sit at the edge of the room. It must sit at the center, from how we benchmark, to how we teach, to how we hire.

If we keep rewarding only goals, we will keep losing the match on defense.

A personal perspective by Jabess Omane.
